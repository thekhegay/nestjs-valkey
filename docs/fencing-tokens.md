# Fencing tokens

A token is the server's clock in microseconds (about 1.79 × 10^15 in 2026), or one more than the key's last token when that is ahead, so tokens grow per key across releases, expiries and clock steps while the fence lives, and after it expires across a clock that reads less than `fenceRetention` behind. They are safe integers, but too large for a 32-bit column: store them as `bigint`. They are comparable per key only, as the contract asks, and they do not start at 1.

The fence matters only for a clock that steps back. While it lives, tokens grow whatever the clock does; once it has expired, a server clock (or, after a failover, the new primary's) that steps back by more than `fenceRetention` past the last token hands out a smaller one. The default, a minute, is many times what a clock under NTP steps back (a leap second, a correction of a second or so). A fence lost whole in an asynchronous failover is a different case that no retention covers. Raise it where a clock can be minutes off: no NTP, a hardware clock kept in local time, VMs restored from a snapshot.

Every key acquired within `fenceRetention` keeps its fence, and `noeviction` never frees one early. Measured as `used_memory` growth over 100 000 fences named `locks:{<uuid>}:fence` (50 bytes): 108 bytes each on Valkey 8.1, 124 on Valkey 9.1, 141 on Valkey 7.2 and Redis 7.0 (`MEMORY USAGE` reports 80 to 96: it leaves out the key's share of the keyspace and expiry tables); a longer prefix or key adds its length. An application that locks 1 000 distinct keys a second holds 60 000 fences at the default, 6.5 to 8.5 MB, and 3.6 million at an hour, 390 to 510 MB. Locks on a few fixed names (`@Locked('jobs:export')`) cost a few kilobytes either way.
